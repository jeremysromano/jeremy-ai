AI · What I believe

Automate the work. Keep the judgment.

The hard part of AI products isn't the model. It's deciding which steps a machine should take, where a person must decide, and how to make that review fast and trustworthy. That's a design problem.

Belief 1

Design the human gate, not just the model.

Every AI workflow needs clear points where a person approves, edits or rejects, with the source, the change and the history in view. If review is slow, people skip it. If it's fast, they trust it.

Belief 2

AI advises the expert. The expert advises the customer.

AI reads intent, checks eligibility and lays out trade-offs. The professional brings judgment and the relationship. The best experiences make the expert better, not invisible.

Belief 3

Start with the person's goal, and remember it.

The goal is the center, not the form. With permission, AI carries context across every interaction, so people stop repeating themselves.

"Here's what we already know. Tell us what changed."

Belief 4

Trust is a UX problem.

Show where an answer came from. Show what changed. Make it easy to undo. People stay in the loop for escalations, judgment and final decisions, not for checking the checker.

AI-first, not AI bolted on. I'm a product and design leader, not an ML researcher. My job is deciding where AI belongs in the workflow, and where it doesn't.

Case study · Phoenix Burst · PhoenixTeam

A GenAI product that reads regulation so people don't have to.

Phoenix Burst watches regulatory sources, works out what each change means and who it affects, and drafts the work. People review and approve at every step that matters.

The problem

Regulated companies get a constant flow of new rules, bulletins and guide updates. Analysts read each one, compare it with the old version, decide what it affects and write requirements by hand. One year of change took nearly a thousand hours just for the impact analysis.

My role · Head of Product Strategy, 2024–2026

I shaped discovery and product vision, found enterprise use cases with Wells Fargo and Cenlar, and designed the human-in-the-loop review and exception workflows. I used AI-assisted discovery, structured prompts and rapid prototyping in code to explore and test the interaction design.

The workflow: machines do the reading, people make the calls

Each step is either automated or a named human gate. The design work was making each gate fast: the source text, the change and the AI's draft side by side, with one-click approve, edit and history.

  1. Detect

    A crawler finds the new rule. No one has to kick it off.

  2. Approve the impact

    AI drafts the summary and impact. A person approves it.

  3. Approve the change

    Each changed statement and citation is reviewed against the source.

  4. Generate and track

    Approved changes become requirements, stories and tests, then go through implementation and monitoring.

The outcome

A year of regulatory change, in hours instead of months

PhoenixTeam reports that impact analysis for one year of change took at least 972 hours by hand. Burst processes the same work, from impact through implementation, in about 37 hours, or about 133 hours including human review.

Selected work

The four habits, in practice.

Earlier work at Guild and PHH, both large lenders. The industry was financial services; the problems were universal: fragmented systems, handoffs between people, and customers who didn't know what happened next.

01 / See the work · Research

We followed one live case for 64 days and logged every touch

Interviews, shadowing, time-and-motion and click-path studies, plus an audit of 15 years of tools. The finding: the biggest delays were handoffs between people. No single screen fix would solve that, so we redesigned around the tasks people actually do.

02 / Make it tangible · Prototyping

From sketch to a workspace people asked for

I start with the roughest version that gets us to the next decision, then raise fidelity as the questions get sharper. Here, a field-heavy legacy screen became a role-based workspace that showed status, priority and the next action.

03 / Scale it · Design systems

One design system, from tokens to full screens

Tokens → components → patterns → templates → features. Twelve product teams and 100+ developers built from the same parts. The same approach moved 100+ one-off client websites onto one governed, responsive template: up to 5× page views and ~2× lead conversion on selected sites.

04 / The whole relationship · Omnichannel

The right message for the moment, on every channel

One message carried through SMS, email, web and mobile, tied to what was actually happening for the customer: paid, due or late. Digital adoption tripled across 200K+ accounts, and print, mail and call volume dropped with it.

When · payment posts

Received

Confirm receipt. Remove uncertainty.

"We've got it. Your payment posted on [date]."
When · due date passes

Past due

Acknowledge the issue. Surface help.

"Your payment is late. We're here to help, and you have options."
When · before the due date

Due

Clarify amount and timing. One clear action.

"Your [amount] payment is due [date]."
